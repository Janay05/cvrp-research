THE MEASUREMENT I HAD BEEN MISSING

Every comparison I've reported so far ran each solver to *its own* stopping
point and then compared costs. That answers "who does better in their own
time" — it does not answer "who does better per unit of time". The correct
test is iso-quality: how much time does each solver need to reach a given
solution quality?

I built FILO2 with its time-limit flag and swept its budget on
Valle-D'Aosta (single core):

  FILO2 budget    Cost
  -----------------------------
  3 s             21,932,131    (log: "COREOPT for 0 seconds" — this is
  5 s             21,932,131     construction + route-min only, no search)
  10 s            21,824,646
  20 s            21,790,302
  40 s            21,766,836
  102 s           21,738,409

Against that, our solver's best result *ever recorded* — the lowest cost
across every configuration, seed, time budget and core count in every
benchmark we have — is 21,923,585 (102 s, 2 cores, verified feasible).

So: FILO2 beats our all-time best result in 10 seconds on a single core.
Ours consumed 204 core-seconds; FILO2's took 10. Roughly 20x less compute
for a 0.45% better answer.

At Lazio (~1M customers) it is worse, not better — and I'd assumed scale was
where we'd look strongest. Pinning FILO2 to our own 315 s wall-clock:

                                                  Cost            Routes
  ------------------------------------------------------------------------
  FILO2, initial construction (no optimization)   3,177,770,000   40,252
  FILO2, final @ 315 s                            3,159,235,192   40,111
  Ours, 315 s, 4 cores                            3,182,981,663   40,431

FILO2's *construction heuristic alone*, before any optimization runs at all,
beats our entire 315-second 4-core pipeline on both cost and route count.

A CORRECTION TO MY LAST UPDATE

I reported that the parallel multi-start portfolio (best-of-12) closed the
gap from 1.20% to 1.04%. That comparison was wrong, and in our favour: I
compared *our* best-of-12 against FILO2's *single-run* mean. FILO2 is also a
seeded randomized solver and is single-threaded, so on the same machine it
gets the identical trick for free. Measured properly, FILO2 best-of-12 =
21,729,621 vs our 21,966,881 — the like-for-like gap is 1.09%, and
multi-start is not a competitive advantage at all. It helps us slightly more
than them only because our seed-to-seed variance is larger, which is itself
a symptom of a less reliable search.

WHY MORE EFFORT WON'T FIX IT

The three levers this architecture has are all exhausted, and I measured
each rather than assuming:

1. More cores — cost is flat. Across a P=1..16 sweep, every configuration
   lands at 22.0-22.15M. P=1, which has *zero* partitioning penalty, is the
   worst point. This directly falsifies the architecture's stated premise
   (from our own design doc) that partitioning converts parallel search
   effort into better cost. The extra cores buy iterations that don't
   convert into quality.

2. More time — cost is flat. Running 4.6x longer (471 s) gave 21,979,044,
   which is still worse than our own 102 s best. We are converged, not
   time-starved. Our quality ceiling is ~21.92M; FILO2's *floor* at 102 s
   is 21.73M. Our ceiling sits above their floor.

3. Better search — four attempts, ~0.014% total. Since the route-min
   experiment I tried adaptive ruin-sizing (cost 35-47% throughput, no
   gain), a move-evaluation cache (correct, but cached the wrong stage), and
   two new local-search operators (+0.014%, kept). All are documented; three
   are disabled in the code.

A CORRECTION TO MY OWN DIAGNOSIS

I should also flag that the root cause I gave in my last report was wrong,
and I only found this out by finally measuring it instead of asserting it.

I had written that our local search does ~103,000 distance evaluations per
iteration "against FILO2's low hundreds", and concluded we simply couldn't
afford enough search. The 103,000 was measured and is real. FILO2's "low
hundreds" was never measured — I assumed it from reading their code. So I
instrumented FILO2's distance function and counted:

  Distance evaluations per search iteration
  -----------------------------------------
  Ours      ~78,300
  FILO2     ~73,900

A ratio of 1.06x — essentially identical, not the ~300x I implied. And on
throughput we are ahead, not behind: ~2,742 iterations/sec/core against
FILO2's ~1,150, and once you normalise for the fact that their iterations
destroy ~23 customers to our ~9, we do about the same search work per core
per second — on 2 cores to their 1. So we deploy roughly twice FILO2's
total search effort and still finish 1.09% behind.

So the binding constraint is not speed, and never was. It's what FILO2 does
with each unit of work:

- Their starting point is roughly our finishing point. FILO2's construction
  + route-minimisation, in about 4 seconds, gives 21,932,131 at
  Valle-D'Aosta; our fully converged best is 21,923,585. At Lazio their
  construction alone beats our final answer. We spend our whole search
  budget arriving roughly where they begin.
- They run 22 operator types including ejection chains (depth ~25); we run
  11 and have no ejection chain. Once the easy moves are gone, they have
  moves available that we don't.

This correction makes the verdict stronger rather than weaker: we cannot
compute our way out, because we already out-compute them and lose. It also
explains point 1 above — throughput is the one thing partitioning buys, and
throughput was never what was holding us back, which is exactly why cost is
flat in P.

And it is why I'd call this a dead end rather than a backlog item. Closing
the gap means adopting FILO2's *algorithm* — their construction, their
ejection chains, their adaptive control. Once we do that, partitioning still
contributes nothing (it only buys throughput, which we've established isn't
the constraint), while continuing to constrain solutions at chunk boundaries
and requiring the whole boundary-healing stage to exist. The end state is
FILO2's algorithm wrapped in a parallel layer that measurably doesn't help —
at which point the thing that makes this architecture ours has been removed
for no gain.

WHAT THE ARCHITECTURE DOES WIN (stated for completeness, not as a
consolation)

- Memory at scale: 3.5 GB vs FILO2's 7.04 GB at Lazio, a 2x win — after I
  found and fixed a bug this week where a disabled feature's buffer was
  still being allocated at full instance size (that fix alone halved our
  footprint, 7.0 GB -> 3.5 GB, with zero behaviour change).
- Predictable wall-clock: we're time-budgeted by construction (103.4-103.6 s
  across seeds) where FILO2's iteration-budgeted design varies 87-194 s.

Neither changes the verdict on the actual aim.

WHERE I'D LIKE YOUR STEER

There is one design that could plausibly win on both axes, but it is a
*different* architecture, not an extension of this one: FILO2-quality global
localized search parallelised by optimistic concurrency (per-route locks,
abort/retry on conflict — each iteration touches ~50 vertices, so conflicts
should be rare). I'd budget 3-4 weeks with real concurrency-correctness
risk, and I would not want to start it without your agreement that it's
worth the time given what we've just learned.
